\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Financial markets move between periods of relative stability and episodes of abrupt repricing, volatility shocks, and severe stress. These changes matter for portfolio construction because the distribution of risky-asset returns is not constant through time. A portfolio that is appropriate during a low-volatility expansion can become poorly calibrated when risk aversion rises, liquidity deteriorates, or volatility accelerates. This creates a natural demand for forward-looking variables that can identify changes in market conditions before they are fully reflected in realized returns.

Option-implied volatility is a natural candidate because it embeds market expectations and the price investors are willing to pay for protection against adverse states. Realized variance measures volatility that has already occurred, whereas implied variance reflects the market price of future uncertainty. Their difference is commonly associated with the Variance Risk Premium (VRP). A positive VRP is economically consistent with persistent demand for crash insurance: buyers of protection pay a premium, while sellers receive compensation for bearing losses when realized variance rises sharply. This mechanism gives VRP two distinct potential uses. It can be treated as information about latent market states, or it can be mapped into a payoff that attempts to harvest the spread between implied and subsequently realized variance.

These two uses are economically different. In the informational channel, VRP-related variables enter a forecasting model. The model estimates the probability of future stress and converts that probability into equity and bond weights. The investment payoff remains the return on an equity--bond portfolio. In the payoff channel, lagged implied variance and subsequent realized variance determine a variance-linked payoff directly. A variable can therefore improve state identification without generating attractive standalone returns. Conversely, a variance-linked payoff can produce positive carry even if the same variable adds little value to a dynamic allocation rule. Treating these channels as equivalent would obscure the central economic question.

This thesis asks: \emph{Does the Variance Risk Premium create more economic value as an informational state variable for dynamic equity--bond allocation or through a model-based direct variance-payoff approximation, and how robust is this distinction across the United States and Europe?} The question is deliberately narrower than asking whether a variance premium exists. A positive average spread between implied and realized variance does not establish that the spread can be transformed into robust investor welfare. The relevant test is whether a strategy survives strong benchmarks, implementation costs, tail risk, parameter changes, and cross-market replication.

The empirical design contains two separate layers. The first studies dynamic equity--bond allocation. Buy-and-hold equity, 60/40, and equal-weighted equity--bond portfolios provide transparent benchmarks. Against them, the thesis evaluates Hidden Markov Models, Markov-switching regressions, and machine-learning stress classifiers. The models use realized variance, implied variance, and alternative VRP transformations to estimate future stress. They are evaluated with rolling out-of-sample procedures. Performance is assessed through return, volatility, Sharpe and Sortino ratios, drawdown, tail risk, turnover, and certainty-equivalent welfare. The aligned allocation samples contain 184 observations in the United States and 122 in Europe.

The second layer studies a model-based direct variance-payoff approximation. The strike proxy is lagged implied variance derived from VIX or VSTOXX, while settlement is represented by the subsequent annualized trailing realized-variance measure. The normalized short-variance payoff is scaled using strictly lagged payoff-volatility estimates and is subject to notional caps. Costs are charged on the full absolute notional initiated each month because the one-month exposure settles and must be renewed. The direct-variance samples contain 232 observations in the United States and 170 in Europe. This layer is not a reconstructed variance-swap backtest. It does not recover an exact option-replicated strike, contractual variance notional, collateral account, margin path, dealer bid--ask spread, or daily mark-to-market process. The resulting return series is therefore a model-based mapping of a variance payoff into portfolio capital, not an observed return on invested capital.

The results show that the two channels have materially different economic properties. The allocation evidence is modest. In the United States, HMM RV + Log VRP reaches a Sharpe ratio of 1.019, compared with 1.025 for the equal-weighted equity--bond benchmark. It improves maximum drawdown but requires substantially more turnover. In Europe, the strongest HMM or RSM specification reaches a Sharpe ratio of 0.281, while buy-and-hold equity reaches 0.313. Machine-learning models provide incremental prediction information in selected specifications, especially the European Random Forest with VRP, but do not establish statistically significant welfare gains over simple benchmarks. The informational channel is therefore relevant, but its economic value is limited and model dependent.

The direct-variance evidence is more asymmetric. In the United States, the selected High-VRP strategy generates a Sharpe ratio of 0.882. Its certainty-equivalent differences relative to 60/40 and equal weighting are negative at a risk-aversion coefficient of five, and the bootstrap confidence intervals include zero. The strategy therefore does not establish robust welfare dominance. In Europe, the strategy that takes exposure only when lagged VRP is positive generates an annualized return of 13.08%, annualized volatility of 9.70%, and a Sharpe ratio of 1.324. Its mean--variance certainty-equivalent advantage is approximately 927 basis points relative to 60/40 and 899 basis points relative to equal weighting. The lower bootstrap confidence bounds remain positive against both benchmarks.

Robustness analysis strengthens this distinction rather than eliminating it. The European direct-variance result remains economically strong under transaction costs from 0 to 50 basis points, volatility-estimation windows from 12 to 60 months, a maximum notional cap reduced to 5%, alternative subperiods, major-crisis exclusions, and risk-aversion coefficients from 1 to 10. The United States result is less stable, particularly under short risk-estimation windows, and does not produce consistent welfare gains. For dynamic allocation, no-trade bands and partial rebalancing improve implementability, but they do not turn the regime models into dominant benchmark alternatives.

The main contribution of the thesis is therefore not the identification of a universally superior VRP strategy. It is the empirical separation of predictive information from payoff design. The same underlying variance variables can have different economic value depending on how they are used. The thesis also imposes benchmark and implementation discipline across two markets and multiple model families. The central finding is that the economic value of the Variance Risk Premium depends critically on the payoff structure through which it is harvested. In the tested European sample, the model-based direct variance-payoff approximation provides the strongest evidence of economic value. In the United States, neither the allocation channel nor the direct-payoff channel establishes robust investor-welfare dominance.
