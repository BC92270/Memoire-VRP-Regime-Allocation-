\begin{abstract}

This thesis examines whether the Variance Risk Premium (VRP) creates more economic value as an informational state variable for dynamic equity--bond allocation or through a model-based direct variance-payoff approximation. The analysis compares the United States and Europe and separates the two channels because they rely on different payoff structures.

The allocation layer evaluates benchmarks against Hidden Markov Models, Markov-switching regressions, and machine-learning stress classifiers. VRP-related variables provide incremental information but do not establish robust economic dominance. In the United States, the strongest HMM reaches a Sharpe ratio of 1.019, compared with 1.025 for the equal-weighted equity--bond benchmark. In Europe, the strongest HMM or RSM reaches 0.281, below buy-and-hold equity at 0.313.

The direct-variance results are more asymmetric. The selected United States High-VRP strategy produces a Sharpe ratio of 0.882 but no statistically significant welfare advantage over the benchmarks. In Europe, the strategy active only when lagged VRP is positive produces an annualized return of 13.08%, volatility of 9.70%, and a Sharpe ratio of 1.324. At a risk-aversion coefficient of five, its mean--variance certainty-equivalent advantage is approximately 927 basis points relative to 60/40 and 899 basis points relative to equal weighting, with positive bootstrap lower bounds.

The main conclusion is that the economic value of VRP depends critically on the payoff structure through which it is harvested. European direct-variance evidence is stronger, while neither channel establishes robust welfare dominance in the United States. The direct-variance layer remains a model-based approximation rather than a reconstructed variance-swap backtest.

\end{abstract}
